Euler Spiral of Elements

All 118 elements laid on one continuous spiral that winds out from an empty null point. Each turn is one energy shell, and the angle is a phase: elements that share a phase share their chemistry. Tap any element.

How the spiral is built

The spiral follows the order in which electrons actually fill their orbitals, the Madelung rule: subshells fill in order of n + l, and within a tie, lower n first. Each full turn of the spiral is one value of n + l, from 1 at the centre to 8 at the rim. That gives turns holding 2, 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32 and 32 elements, the arrangement known as Janet's left-step table, here wound into a circle.

Every turn is divided into the same 32 angular slots: 14 for the f block, 10 for d, 6 for p and 2 for s. An element's slot is the place its last electron takes inside its subshell. Because the slots line up from turn to turn, chemical families fall on straight spokes. The alkali metals sit on one spoke, the halogens on another, and the noble gases from neon down on a third. Helium lands on the alkaline-earth spoke, because its outer subshell is a filled s pair, the same as beryllium's.

The two dashed slots on the outermost turn are 8s, the places for elements 119 and 120, which have not been made.

Where the picture strains

Madelung order is an approximation. About twenty elements, chromium, copper, palladium, gold and most of the early actinides among them, put their electrons somewhere slightly different, and the panel shows the measured configuration when it differs. The spiral places lanthanum and actinium in the f block, as Janet does; other tables put them in d. The book reading in the right-hand column is interpretation: ontological mathematics names the analogy but does not derive the periodic table from it.
